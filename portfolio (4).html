About

I've spent the last several years working on the parts of software most people never see: the queues, the retries, the migrations that have to run at 3am without anyone noticing. I like systems that are boring in production and interesting to build.

Before backend work, I studied computer science and spent a couple of years doing freelance web development around Bandung — which is mostly where I learned that a fast demo and a system that survives real traffic are two very different things.

Outside of work I mentor junior engineers, contribute to a couple of open-source CLI tools, and am slowly rewriting a side project in Rust for no good reason other than wanting to.

How I think about tools now

I don't hold tightly to a fixed stack anymore. Go, Rust, and TypeScript are the constants, but the rest changes by the project — with an AI pair to learn fast, picking up an unfamiliar language or a new part of the infra isn't the blocker it used to be. What matters more than any one tool is knowing what to build and why.

So if you ask about my experience, I'll usually answer with what I've actually shipped rather than what's listed on a résumé — this year alone that's included Zig, a small WASM plugin runtime, and pgvector, each picked up mid-project with an AI doing a lot of the heavy lifting on the parts I hadn't touched before.

"Good systems are boring. That's the whole point — the interesting part happened months before anyone noticed it working." — something I tell every junior engineer I mentor

How I approach a new system

The shape barely changes, even when the problem does. It keeps me from over-engineering on day one and under-engineering by month three.

  1. Write down what "on fire" looks like before writing any code
  2. Design for the failure mode, not the happy path
  3. Ship the boring version first
  4. Add complexity only when the data asks for it

2023 — Present · Nimbus Systems

Senior Backend Engineer

Leading the payments infrastructure team.

I joined to help stabilize a payments platform that was growing faster than its infrastructure could handle. The biggest problem was reconciliation — thousands of transactions a minute across five providers, with no reliable way to catch mismatches until finance found them days later.

Over eighteen months we rebuilt that pipeline from the ground up, and I ended up owning the internal platform team as well, since every reliability problem kept tracing back to how services were provisioned in the first place.

  • Rebuilt the reconciliation pipeline to handle 40k+ transactions per second with sub-minute mismatch detection
  • Migrated the core services to a new provisioning platform (Wharf) with zero planned downtime
  • Grew the platform team from two people to six, and wrote the onboarding playbook they still use

2021 — 2023 · Arta Digital

Backend Engineer

Owned the order-processing service for a fast-growing logistics product.

Arta Digital's order system was a single Rails monolith handling everything from inventory to driver dispatch, and it was starting to fall over during peak hours. My job was to pull the order-processing logic out into its own service without breaking anything customers depended on.

The migration ran alongside the existing monolith for four months, with traffic shifted over gradually and a rollback path kept ready at every stage. It was also where I gave my first conference talk, on what that gradual-migration process actually looked like in practice.

  • Extracted order-processing into an independent service with no planned downtime during cutover
  • Cut average order-confirmation latency significantly during peak hours
  • Spoke at a local meetup about strangler-pattern migrations in production
